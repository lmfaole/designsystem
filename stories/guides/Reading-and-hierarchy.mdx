# Reading and hierarchy

A page is read, not looked at. Most of what the design system shows is **text in a list**: names, states, dates. The reader's
job is to find what matters and act. Everything here is about making that job as short as possible.

## One direction: down

What is read stands **under each other, in one column**. Not in a grid.

A grid of equal columns makes the reader read **across, then down, then across again**. That has three costs:

1. **The order is in doubt.** Is the second item to the right of the first, or under it? The reader has to work out the order
   before reading, and may get it wrong.
2. **What comes first is hidden.** In a column the first thing is at the top and the reader knows it is first. In a grid the first
   thing is one of several things in the top row.
3. **The eye does more.** It crosses the width of the page for every row, and a wide screen makes it worse.

So the design system has **one reading column** (`PageLayout width="reading"`, the default): 48rem, centred. On a wide screen the
rest is **margin, not more columns**: that is not wasted space, it is what keeps the line short enough to follow.

**Columns are for what is looked at, not read:** pictures (`Tiles`), charts, cards of equal weight, a few figures or chips, and
a `Split` rail of *reference* (a cover and its facts) beside the main content. A page that is mostly such things is
`width="wide"`.

## Order is priority

Top to bottom is **most to least important**, the same on a phone and on a desktop. The layout never reorders by convenience.
If two things matter equally, one still goes first.

The **first thing the eye lands on** is the one that matters most on the screen. Not two.

## Three levels, visibly different

Page title, section title, item. Each differs from the next in size, weight or case, so the structure can be seen without reading.
Do not add a fourth level of emphasis: `destructive` and bold are for what **stops** something, and the more they are used, the less
they say.

## Space groups; lines and boxes do not

Things that belong together are **close** (`xs`, `sm`); groups are **further apart** (`lg`, `xl`). The space between two sections
is always larger than the space inside one, so a group is seen as a group without a line round it. Rules and cards are for the rare
thing that must be set apart.

## One left edge, a short eye path

Everything starts at the same left edge, so the eye can run down it. A row's date or count is at the end of the **same row**, and
the row is no wider than 48rem, so the eye does not have to cross a whole screen from a name to its date. Prose is 45–75
characters wide (`max-w-prose`).

## Rows are scanned down the left

Each row starts with the same thing (the kind, then the name). The **name is the strongest part** of a row; what else is on it is
`muted` and small. All rows of a list have the same structure, so the reader learns it once.

## Do not make the reader choose a path

One column is one path. Do not put two lists side by side to be compared (that is a table), and do not hide the rest behind tabs
when it fits under. A reader who has to choose where to start has already spent attention.

## Short before long

A summary and the few that matter come first; the long list comes after, or on a page of its own (see **Pages and navigation**).

## Checking

1. Look at the page at **400, 1440 and 2560 px**. At every width the reader must be able to read straight down.
2. Cover everything but the first screen. Is it clear what matters most?
3. Squint. Can the three levels still be told apart? Is anything shouting that should not be?
4. Follow the left edge with your eye. Does it say what each row is?

## Don't

- Don't use `Grid` for text, lists or the sections of a page (it is for what is looked at).
- Don't put a rail beside the main content to carry a second list.
- Don't centre text or lists inside the column; the left edge is the guide.
- Don't use bold, red or a box to make something stand out unless it stops something.
